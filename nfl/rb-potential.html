Project Overview and Goal

The running back chart applies the same general concept as the receiver chart: compare a player's current fantasy production with the production typically associated with his role, then evaluate whether that difference can identify a future change in scoring. However, running backs receive opportunities differently, requiring a separate index for their involvement in the offense.

I used an index combining carries, targets, and touchdowns, then compared it with fantasy points per game in 1 point-per-reception scoring. A running back below the regression line received positive potential, while one above it received negative potential. The goal was to determine whether these differences could identify players whose scoring would improve or decline.

The first-year results show that the decline selections were much more successful than the rebound selections. Eight of nine Least Potential selections declined, while only two of nine Most Potential selections improved. The volume leaders and green box also identified high later scorers, though high average production did not always mean consistent scoring in every game.

Year-One Results

The original RB charts were published after Weeks 7 through 16 of the 2025 regular season. I evaluated the selections against the next three scheduled games of each player's team, excluding byes. The following results use average PPR points per recorded appearance unless a team-game measure is specifically stated. This review was completed after the season, using the selections that had already appeared on the published charts.

Numerical scoring error

The regression line was also evaluated as an estimate of future scoring. Its mean absolute error was 4.51 PPR points per appearance, compared with 4.33 when using the player's current average. Balanced direction accuracy, which gives increases and declines equal weight, was 51.1%. In this first-year test, using the line did not improve numerical scoring accuracy over the current-average baseline.

Methodology and Definitions

The Volume Per Game Index

A running back's involvement cannot be evaluated through carries alone, as targets contribute to fantasy scoring through both receptions and receiving yards. The original Volume Per Game Index therefore combined carries and targets with a weighted touchdown term. The formula used for these charts is shown below.

Volume Per Game Index = (targets + carries + 4.5 × total touchdowns) / recorded player games
Chart y = full-PPR fantasy points / recorded player games

The touchdown term reflects a component of production that is especially important to a running back's fantasy scoring. However, touchdowns also directly contribute to the scoring being plotted on the vertical axis. The index therefore combines opportunity and realized production, rather than measuring workload alone. Air yards are not part of this original formula.

Potential and the Regression Line

A least-squares line describes the relationship between the index and current scoring among the plotted running backs. Potential is the fitted scoring value minus the player's current scoring average. It is the negative of the usual regression residual.

Potential = fitted PPR per game - current PPR per game

For example, a running back averaging 14 points whose index corresponds to 17 on the line would have +3 potential. The score describes a gap in the current relationship. Whether that gap correctly identifies later improvement must be evaluated using future games, which is the purpose of the year-one results above.

How the Results Were Evaluated

Each eligible chart selection was compared with scoring in the next three scheduled games of the player's cutoff team. A window is one player evaluated from one chart, so the same running back can appear in several windows. Improvement means the later average exceeded the average at publication; decline means it was lower. Ties do not count as a correct change.

Appearance-based averages use games with recorded player statistics. Team-game measures also include games without a later player appearance, counting those as zero. Byes do not count as games, and windows without three remaining regular-season games or the required same-team follow-up are excluded from the primary three-game test.

What the Boxes Showed

The green and yellow boxes were evaluated using the rectangles that appeared on each original chart. A player was included when the center of his dot fell inside the published rectangle. The boundaries were measured from the charts rather than replaced with new boundaries chosen after the results were known.

Notable Individual Predictions

Saquon Barkley: Most Potential after Week 7

Barkley averaged 13.49 PPR points per appearance when selected. His later three-game average was 18.83, an increase of approximately 5.35 points using the unrounded values. This was a successful rebound selection, but it was one of only two successful Most Potential windows.

Quinshon Judkins: Repeated Most Potential Selections

Judkins was selected on eight consecutive charts after Weeks 8 through 15. He improved in only one of those follow-up windows, and that improvement was 1.44 points per appearance. The repeated selections had a large impact on the overall rebound result and show that a positive gap can persist without a corresponding increase in scoring.

Jonathan Taylor: Least Potential after Week 10

Taylor was averaging 27.39 points when selected, while his later average was 11.70. His decline of 15.69 points followed the direction identified by the chart. Even so, a decline from an exceptionally high average should be interpreted separately from a claim that the player has little remaining fantasy value.

Christian McCaffrey: Highest Volume

All nine evaluated Highest Volume selections were McCaffrey. He remained in the future scoring top five in each window and averaged 24.43 later points per appearance across those selections. This was a strong result for his season, but the repeated selections do not show that the same outcome would occur across nine different running backs.

Conclusions and Limitations

The first year provides stronger support for the published decline warnings than for the rebound calls. The Most Potential selections improved less frequently than their random-player baseline, and the broader signal also fell below the always-decline baseline. The results therefore do not support using positive potential alone as a reliable predictor of an increase in scoring.

Please note that the nine Most Potential selections involved only two distinct players. After removing identical player and endpoint windows caused by byes, the result was two improvements in eight windows. Counting only the first selection of each distinct player produced one improvement in two selections. These small samples limit how broadly the findings can be applied.

The index includes touchdowns, and a change in touchdown frequency can affect both the index and scoring. Injuries, changes in the backfield, and changes in offensive role can also alter production in ways the line does not account for. The fitted relationship describes the current dataset; it does not establish that a player's efficiency will return to the league relationship.

Finally, the Least Potential selections still averaged 17.04 later points per appearance. Correctly predicting that scoring would decline is different from establishing that a player would stop producing at a useful level.

Did each arrow identify the literal chart maximum or minimum?

This is a separate check of the label at publication, rather than a future scoring result. Among all displayed dots, Most Potential identified the residual leader on 9 of 10 charts (90.0%). Least Potential, Highest Production, and Highest Volume each identified the corresponding chart extreme on all 10 charts. A 0.01-point tolerance was used for rounding.

The forward-scoring tables have fewer evaluated selections because charts after Week 16 do not have three remaining regular-season games. Label accuracy at publication should not be interpreted as future prediction accuracy.
